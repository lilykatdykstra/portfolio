Wood + Acrylic Shelf

Overview

A hand-fabricated shelf that pairs walnut-stained wood with welded acrylic inserts — two materials that don't usually share space but turn out to complement each other well. The project was an exercise in precision joinery, surface finishing, and learning a new bonding process: solvent welding acrylic instead of relying on mechanical fasteners.

Concept & Sketches

The brief was open-ended: test two materials in combination and produce something functional. I gravitated toward wood and acrylic because the contrast — warm grain against cold transparency — felt interesting to resolve structurally. Early sketches worked through how to seat the acrylic panels within the wood frame without adhesive failure or visible gaps.

Materials & Tools

Wood & Finishing

Dimensional lumber, walnut stain, sandpaper (80–220 grit)

Woodshop Tools

Table saw, chop saw, router, clamps, mallet

Acrylic

Clear acrylic sheet, solvent cement (IPS Weld-On), applicator needle

Process

The shelves were cut from lumber on the table saw and trimmed to length on the chop saw. Miter joints were marked and cut carefully — miter joinery depends entirely on angle consistency, and any drift compounds across the assembly. Shelf boards were dry-fit before committing to anything.

Staining came next. I applied a mid-tone walnut stain by hand and let it fully cure before moving on — trying to weld acrylic onto a freshly stained surface would have introduced contamination at the bond line.

The acrylic inserts were cut to fit the routed channels in each shelf level, then welded with solvent cement. Acrylic welding works by capillary action: a small amount of solvent wicks into the joint and partially dissolves both surfaces, which then fuse as the solvent evaporates. Getting it right required keeping the joint aligned under light pressure and working quickly before the solvent flashed off.

Assembly

Leveling & Sanding

Once fully assembled, the shelf was wobbly — the dowel joinery had introduced small inconsistencies that threw off the level. I worked through several fixes: sanding and adjusting the legs, then sawing them down with a Japanese saw to equalize the contact. Failed dowel holes were filled with wood filler. Ultimately, rubber feet on the base brought everything into stable balance.

For surface finishing, I used an orbital sander on the acrylic panels for consistency, and hand-sanded the wooden frames up to 120 grit. The acrylic sanding also helped soften any imperfections at the welded joints, giving them a subtle frosted quality. After prepping the surfaces, I applied stain and let everything cure before calling the piece done.

Reflection

I'm hard on myself, and going in I wanted the piece to be more perfect. But looking at the finished shelf, I can see how much the process itself taught me — the sanding, staining, and acrylic finishing transformed materials that had a lot of small problems into something I genuinely liked.

I also learned which techniques I'd approach differently. Dowels were the main thing: they introduced just enough variability to make leveling a real challenge. But I also came away with a better instinct for creative problem-solving — when the legs wouldn't sit flat, rubber feet turned what felt like a compromise into a clean functional solution.
