Lamp-mania

Overview

Lamp-mania is a series of three hand-built lamps, each using a completely different material and construction approach. The series was a chance to slow down and ask a specific question with every build: what does this material do to light, and what forms does it make possible?

The lamps — Mushroom, Flower, and Jellyfish — each started from a natural form and were worked out in sketches before anything was built. Materials were chosen to serve the form, not the other way around.

Lamp 01 — Mushroom

The mushroom lamp is built around a carved and shaped cap shade that sits over a simple stem base. The cap's gentle curve diffuses the light softly around the rim while the underside glows — mimicking the way light filters through a real mushroom's gills. The form is deceptively simple; most of the work went into getting the proportions right so it reads as mushroom-like without being cartoonish.

Lamp 02 — Flower

The flower lamp is a pendant made from individually shaped and sewn fabric petals, each built around a wire armature. The petals are wired together so they open outward like a bloom and hang naturally under gravity. Fabric and wire are not materials I had worked with before, and the construction — shaping wire, sewing fabric over it, assembling petal by petal — was slow and iterative in the best way.

The light source sits at the center, and the fabric diffuses the warmth outward. The form only comes together when it's lit.

Lamp 03 — Jellyfish

The jellyfish lamp hangs from a cord and uses trailing elements to mimic the tentacles of a jellyfish in motion. The goal was to create something that felt alive — a shade that catches air movement and shifts slightly when you walk past it.

Reflection

Three lamps, three completely different skill sets. The mushroom lamp was about form and proportion; the flower lamp was about patience and material discovery (I had never sewn anything before this); the jellyfish was about movement and creating something that responds to its environment rather than just sitting still.

The consistent thread across all three was starting from a natural form and figuring out which materials could carry it. Form first, then material, then technique — that sequence made every decision easier.
